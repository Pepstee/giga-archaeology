# Art of Sales: Notes from Five Months in Direct Sales

## Preface

I took these notes over five months in direct sales. Some came from people in Birmingham,
some from office visits and conference calls, and some from professionals elsewhere in the
country or abroad. I wrote down slogans, pitch structures, objections, training systems and
anything else that appeared to explain why one person performed and another did not.

When I first assembled the book, I wanted it to be a memorial to my time at Saleshub and
Appco. I was grateful for the skills and wanted to preserve them before moving on to my own
business. That is still true, but it is incomplete. The same system that taught me to speak to
strangers and measure my performance also knew how to use a person's dreams against them.

This book is therefore both a manual and an account. It records the techniques as I learnt
them, explains what they were supposed to do and points out where discipline turned into
pressure or persuasion turned into manipulation.

## 1. What direct sales actually sells

The office taught that every business needs exposure and clients. Indirect methods included
billboards, television, radio, leaflets and the internet. Direct methods meant meeting or
calling people. The three common environments were B2B, residential and events.

Performance pay made the opportunity attractive. More effort could produce more money and
recognition. The progression story moved from employee to self-employed salesperson, business
owner and investor. In practice, the same story could be used to challenge any limit a worker
set. A day off became evidence that the person did not really want a team. Leaving at 17:30
became evidence that the person did not really want the phone or lifestyle they had named.

The opportunity was real, but so was the leverage. Anyone entering this industry should learn
to tell the difference.

## 2. The field mindset

The office reduced the mindset to three commandments: work ethic, student mentality and a
positive attitude. The useful part was personal control. You could not control the weather,
the site or a stranger's answer, but you could control preparation, effort, delivery and the
number of attempts.

"Detach your money from your approach" was one of the best notes. Chasing the next commission
made the pitch desperate. Focusing on habits, knowledge, skill and mentality made it easier to
stay calm. The office called that indifference: relaxed confidence rather than apathy.

The danger was turning the method into an identity test. "No days off" and "first in, last
out" can describe a short push, but they are not evidence of character. A good system measures
performance without demanding that the worker become the system.

## 3. The five-step pitch

The Cycle of Development named five parts: introduction, presentation, short story, close and
rehash. The introduction earns a few seconds. The presentation states the problem and offer.
The short story gives the customer a person or situation to picture. The close asks for a
decision. The rehash continues after the first answer when there is a genuine question to
resolve.

SEE meant smile, eye contact and enthusiasm. PQA meant point, question and agreement. These
were tools for attention and participation. Tonality carried the emotional shape: lower and
more serious on the problem, brighter on the solution. Delivery still had to be loud, clear,
relaxed and slow enough to understand.

## 4. Probability and review

The law of averages removed some of the drama from rejection. Ten attempts could produce
nothing. One hundred attempts gave skill and probability more room to work. The doctrine was
not that every person was interchangeable. It was that a salesperson should not prejudge who
might engage.

Numbers only help if they lead to review. The notes say to record what happened after a deal,
cross-reference the data and ask three questions: what did I ask, what did I do well and what
needs improvement? Volume without review repeats the same mistake faster.

## 5. Objections and closes

The training divided objections into blocks, excuses and concerns. Feel Felt Found answered a
concern by acknowledging it, describing someone who had felt the same and explaining what
that person later found. Another sequence was agree, acknowledge, ignore, resume and close.

These methods can stop a salesperson from becoming defensive. They can also make the
salesperson treat every refusal as a puzzle to defeat. A concern can be explored. A clear no
should remain a no.

## 6. Learning faster

The best students asked questions, took notes, practised and spent time with their teams. They
became information seekers rather than waiting to receive whatever the office happened to
teach. Development happened in the field, in meetings, on office visits, through conference
calls and in one-to-one conversations.

The skill gap was the distance between current performance and the imagined perfect
salesperson. The useful response was to define the missing behaviour, practise it and review
evidence. "Work harder" is not a diagnosis.

## 7. Training and culture

A training system must be teachable and copyable. New people need a correct reference from the
beginning. The notes propose in-house training, breakfast meetings, social nights, calls,
office visits and road trips as the person develops.

Culture can improve morale and make hard days tolerable. It can also make people afraid to
leave. Fake scarcity in interviews, assigning a CV a number to imply other candidates and
making the interviewee feel inferior are not leadership techniques. They are theatre.

## 8. Goals, value and progression

The notes split weekly goals into sales, development, personal and career goals. Each one
needs an owner, a written record and a review point. The eight daily steps were positive
attitude, time management, preparation, full effort, knowing the site, taking action, having a
target and taking control.

"Your value is your result" was useful when it meant that status and talk could not replace
performance. It became dangerous when human value was collapsed into sales numbers. The
better reading is narrower: professional claims require evidence.

## 9. Two pitches from the field

The GOSH and Acorns pitches show the method in practice. Both open quickly, establish a local
or recognised organisation, describe a problem, make the solution physical and ask for a
small recurring commitment. Details such as a school, family accommodation, a rooftop garden,
overnight stays and a farm give the listener something concrete to picture.

The pitches also create pressure through agreement questions, social proof, price comparisons
and fear of a child being turned away. Those techniques must be used with accurate figures and
without manufacturing consent. Emotion belongs in the pitch because the subject is emotional.
That does not excuse exaggeration.

## Conclusion

Direct sales gave me useful skills. It taught me to approach people, control my delivery,
recover from rejection, study better performers and keep evidence of my work. It also showed
me how easily ambition can be converted into unpaid loyalty to somebody else's system.

Keep the skills. Audit the opportunity. If a company talks more about recruitment, ownership
and lifestyle than the product or customer, find out where the money really comes from. If the
answer depends mainly on bringing in more sellers, paying to enter or buying inventory to keep
your status, leave.

I wrote the original book to preserve what I had learnt. This version preserves something
more useful: what I learnt, what I later questioned and the difference between the two.
