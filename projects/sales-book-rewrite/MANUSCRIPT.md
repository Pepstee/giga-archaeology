# Art of Sales

## Notes from five months in direct sales

## Preface

At 3:52 in the morning on 2 November 2024, I thought the book was finished.

My message said that five months of notes from salespeople around the country and abroad had
been organised into a coherent manual. I wanted to preserve what I had learnt and pass it on.
My time at Saleshub and Appco had ended, I was moving towards my own business, and the book
would serve as a memorial to that period.

That was the honest reason for writing it. It was also too clean.

The original manuscript made direct sales sound like a complete system for becoming more
disciplined, confident and successful. Some of that was true. I learnt how to stop strangers,
hold their attention, ask for a decision and continue after a day full of rejection. I learnt
to watch people who were better than me, take notes and test what they did. I became much less
afraid of uncomfortable conversations.

I also watched an office turn ambition into leverage. If somebody wanted a new phone, a
holiday, a team or a business, that dream could be brought back whenever they wanted to rest.
"Only working until 17:30? I thought you really wanted that new phone." A goal that belonged
to the worker became an argument for giving the office another hour.

Both parts belong in the book.

This is not a general encyclopaedia of sales. It is a field manual reconstructed from the
notes I actually kept between July and November 2024. The phrases are sometimes rough because
the experience was rough. Some ideas are useful. Some are manipulative. A few are both,
depending on what the salesperson does after the customer answers.

I have resisted the urge to fill every gap. If my notes name a framework but do not explain
it, I say so. If a claim came from training, I call it training doctrine rather than dressing
it up as settled science. If a technique works by making someone feel small or confused, I do
not rename it "influence" and move on.

The point is to keep what sales taught me without allowing the sales system to write the
meaning of the experience for me.

## 1. What direct sales actually sells

The first promise was bigger than money.

My notes begin with the idea that a desirable job is not defined only by the amount attached
to it. You might be at the front of a growing business. Your reward might be a career. Show
ambition, set goals and grow with the company.

That promise matters because performance pay is difficult to sell on income alone. Some weeks
are good. Some are not. There is no comfortable exchange of fixed time for fixed money, so the
office sells the future as well as the work.

The progression model in my notes had five levels:

1. Hourly work, associated in the training with part-time or low-skilled jobs.
2. Salary, associated with permanent employment.
3. Performance pay and self-employment, where income depends on skill, application, quantity
   and quality.
4. Ownership, where the work expands into managing people and a company.
5. Investment.

This was not a neutral map of the economy. It was a recruitment story. Each stage was meant
to make the next one appear freer, more valuable and more ambitious. It gave a new seller a
reason to tolerate an unstable present: "The harder you work now, the bigger the payback in
the future."

There is some truth in it. Sales does reward behaviour that a salary can hide. If one person
speaks to twice as many qualified prospects, reviews every interaction and improves their
pitch, the result can move quickly. A person willing to work an eighty-hour week may get
noticed.

But recognition is not the same as a fair return. Nor does willingness prove that eighty
hours were necessary. Before joining any performance-pay business, ask what is being measured,
who owns the customer relationship, who carries the risk and how many people at your level
earn what the recruiter implies.

### The office model of the market

We were taught that every business needs exposure and clients. The office grouped marketing
into indirect methods such as billboards, media, radio, television, leaflets and the internet,
and direct methods such as face-to-face or telephone sales.

The criticism of indirect marketing was simple:

- high upfront and continuing costs;
- no guaranteed return;
- little personal tailoring;
- easy for the customer to ignore;
- weak at creating an immediate impulse.

Direct sales offered a person instead. That person could adjust the message, notice a reaction
and ask for a decision immediately.

The common field environments were B2B, residential and events. In the office shorthand, B2B
meant approaching people at work, residential meant meeting them at home, and events meant
catching them while they were already out. Each environment changes the opening. Someone at
home has privacy but may distrust the interruption. Someone at an event is already in motion
and gives you seconds. A business conversation may require more qualification and a clearer
commercial case.

The product was only part of what direct sales sold. To the client, the agency sold exposure
and customer acquisition. To the customer, the representative sold a campaign or offer. To
the representative, the office sold development, promotion and eventual ownership.

That third sale deserves the same scrutiny as the first two.

### Opportunity language can become a trap

My 4 November notes are less enthusiastic than the July notebook. By then I had written down
how the office could use a person's stated dreams:

> "Want to take a day off? I thought you really wanted to set the pace for your team, grow it,
> recruit more people, be promoted, ultimately get your own company?"

This works because it changes the subject. The practical question might be whether someone is
exhausted, whether the site is worth another day or whether the pay justifies the hours. The
office replies with a question about identity: are you really ambitious?

A sound opportunity survives ordinary questions. How many hours? What costs do I carry? What
is the median outcome? Can I say no to a road trip without damaging my progression? If asking
those questions is treated as a failure of belief, the company is not testing your ambition.
It is protecting its story.

## 2. The field mindset

The three commandments in my notes were work ethic, student mentality and positive attitude.
They appeared everywhere because they cover the three things the office wanted from a new
person: keep moving, accept instruction and do not spread defeat through the team.

As field rules, they are useful. As moral rules, they can become ugly.

### Detach money from the approach

"Detach your money from your approach" may be the most useful sentence in the notebook.

When the next interaction has to pay for the day, the customer can feel it. You speed up. You
force agreement. A pause feels like a threat. The close becomes an attempt to extract relief
from a stranger.

Detachment means putting attention back on what can be executed:

- habits;
- knowledge;
- skill;
- mentality;
- preparation;
- quality and quantity of attempts.

The office used the word indifference. It did not mean that the outcome was irrelevant. It
meant relaxed confidence. Desperation is the opposite. A desperate seller communicates that
they need this person. An indifferent seller can ask directly, hear the answer and continue.

Theo's conference call sharpened the point: the only thing you can ultimately control is
yourself. Gain confidence from knowing that you did everything correctly. Focus on progress
rather than the immediate result. Take your time.

That is a better basis for confidence than pretending rejection does not hurt.

### The perfect salesperson and the impossible worker

One page of the notebook tries to describe the perfect salesperson:

- positive and focused;
- unwilling to settle;
- strong work ethic;
- listens;
- manages time;
- believes in themselves;
- disciplined;
- learns quickly;
- remains a student;
- dresses sharply;
- has thick skin;
- observes without bias;
- carries a "whatever it takes" attitude.

Another list describes successful people as never late, never off, the sharpest dressed, free
of lies and bullshit, organised, competitive, planned for life and aggressive towards success.

Lists like these can focus a person before a field day. They can also make normal limits feel
like defects. "Whatever it takes" does not contain its own stopping rule. Neither does "no days
off".

The better question in the notes is smaller: am I doing enough, for long enough? It can be
answered with evidence. Did I prepare? Did I reach the target number of interactions? Was my
delivery consistent? Did I review the weak section of the pitch? It does not require proving
that I am a hardcore motherfucker by sacrificing every other part of life.

### Self-talk

The notebook records five statements for motivation:

1. I can do a specific number of sales.
2. Sales are easy.
3. I am a high roller.
4. I am a leader.
5. I am a fucking legend.

The last line is funny because it is deliberately excessive. The useful part is not magical
thinking. It is choosing a state before the site chooses one for you. A slow first hour can
become the story of the whole day if you let it. A short reset, a memory of previous good
performance and one controlled interaction can interrupt that story.

Confidence should still return to evidence. Speak to one hundred people with full effort.
Track what happens. The result may be disappointing, but it is real. A slogan can start the
next attempt. It cannot replace the spreadsheet.

## 3. The five-step pitch

The Cycle of Development checklist gives the pitch in five words:

1. Introduction.
2. Presentation.
3. Short story.
4. Close.
5. Rehash.

The five steps are simple enough to remember while standing in noise, watching the next person
approach and trying not to speak too quickly.

### Introduction

The introduction buys attention. At an event, it may be no more than a greeting, the name of
the organisation and a recognition question: "You've heard of us, haven't you?"

First impression begins before the words. My notes separate appearance, how you carry
yourself, energy and body language. The pitch is the message, but the customer receives the
messenger first.

The office compressed this into SEE:

- smile;
- eye contact;
- enthusiasm.

A smile makes an interruption less hostile. Eye contact confirms that the approach is meant
for this person. Enthusiasm gives the other person a reason to believe the next thirty seconds
may be worth hearing. None of these saves a bad proposition. They stop a reasonable one from
dying before it is spoken.

### Presentation

The presentation explains why the conversation is happening. A useful pitch does not begin
with every feature. It identifies a problem, makes it concrete and moves to a solution.

My delivery notes say:

- ask more questions;
- flow like water;
- make the close powerful;
- be confident;
- speak loudly and clearly;
- relax;
- slow down;
- practise.

"Flow like water" was not a licence to ramble. It meant that the structure should survive an
interruption. If the customer answers early, move with the answer. If they already know the
charity, do not recite the recognition section anyway.

Tonality carried the emotional direction. We were taught to go lower and more serious on the
problem, then become visibly brighter on the solution. The change tells the listener how the
parts relate before they have analysed every word.

Training also repeated the 7 percent words, 38 percent tone and 55 percent body-language
ratio. I record it here because it shaped the coaching, not because it should be treated as a
universal law. The safe lesson is ordinary: in face-to-face sales, delivery changes how words
are received.

### Short story

A short story gives the proposition a human scale. In the GOSH pitch, the building was not
only a cancer centre. It included a school so children would not lose their education, family
accommodation so parents could stay, and a rooftop garden and playground where a child could
go outside.

Those details work because "state-of-the-art centre" is a label. A parent staying near their
child is a scene.

The story must remain true. Salespeople are rewarded for emotional force, which creates a
temptation to sharpen every number and remove every qualification. The more emotional the
subject, the stricter the factual discipline should be.

### Close

The close asks for a decision. Do not deliver a strong case and then retreat into "have a
think" because asking feels uncomfortable.

PQA was one method for maintaining participation:

| Part | Function | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| Point | Give one hard piece of information | State the problem or fact |
| Question | Ask a loaded but understandable question | "That is far too high, wouldn't you agree?" |
| Agreement | Notice the verbal or non-verbal response | Confirm and continue |

Agreement questions keep a pitch from becoming a monologue. They also accumulate momentum.
That is exactly why they must be used carefully. Several small agreements do not create
consent to the final payment.

### Rehash

Rehash begins after resistance. The training checklist lists the common residential answers:
no time, partner not home, already do enough, not interested, no bank account and reluctance
to give bank details at the door.

Some are questions. Some are exits. The salesperson's job is to know the difference.

If a person says the partner controls the account, arranging a later conversation may make
sense. If the person does not trust a doorstep payment, explain the process and verification
options. If they simply do not want to participate, repeating the emotional problem in a new
form is not customer service.

## 4. Probability, numbers and honest review

Direct sales is full of emotion, but the work can be measured.

The law of averages in my notes is straightforward. The probability of a result grows as the
number of attempts grows. Ten conversations may produce nothing. A hundred conversations give
the process more chances. Sales can happen at any time, so do not decide in advance that the
next person will say no.

This was partly a defence against prejudice. The seller who silently labels people as poor,
busy, unfriendly or unlikely donors reduces the sample before the customer has done anything.
The field rule was: do not prejudge negatively.

The law of averages does not mean skill is irrelevant. My example note imagined that eighty
out of one hundred people might engage when approached with a smile and enthusiasm, while only
forty might engage without them. The numbers were illustrative. The mechanism was the point:
volume and conversion both matter.

### What to record

The notes repeatedly return to tracking:

- keep sales numbers;
- track interactions;
- use a coaching calendar;
- keep a sales spreadsheet;
- after a sign-up, write down what the customer did;
- cross-reference the data;
- evaluate every experience rather than taking it for granted.

A useful daily record needs at least attempts, meaningful conversations, full pitches, closes,
sign-ups and any later rejection or cancellation that becomes known. Quality of sale matters.
A sign-up that immediately fails paperwork or cancels is not identical to a durable customer.

### Review without mythology

My review structure was:

1. Questions.
2. What was done well.
3. Areas for improvement.

Begin with questions because memory is selective. What exactly did the person say? Where did
they stop making eye contact? Did I speed up in the close? Did the same objection occur three
times? Was it the site, the proposition or my explanation?

Then identify what worked. This matters on bad days. A day with no sign-ups may still contain
a cleaner opening or better control of pace. Small successes can push growth if they are
specific.

Finish with one change that can be practised. "Be more confident" is too broad. "Pause after
the first agreement question and lower my speed in the funding section" can be tested on the
next five pitches.

The law of averages protects the salesperson from overreacting to a small sample. It should
not protect a poor pitch from evidence.

## 5. Objections, closes and consent

The October notes divide objections into blocks, excuses and concerns.

A block is a practical barrier. The person may not control the account or may genuinely have
no time. A concern is a question about trust, price, commitment or suitability. "Excuse" is
the dangerous category because it encourages the salesperson to assume that the customer's
stated reason is not real.

### Feel Felt Found

Feel Felt Found, or the 3F technique, gives a non-defensive response:

| Step | What the salesperson does | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Feel | Acknowledge the concern | Show that the person was heard |
| Felt | Refer to someone with a similar concern | Reduce isolation and defensiveness |
| Found | Explain the relevant outcome or fact | Offer a reason to reconsider |

The method works when the comparison is true and relevant. It fails when "other people felt
the same" becomes a fabricated story pasted onto every objection.

Another sequence in the notebook was agree, acknowledge, ignore, resume and close. The word
"ignore" should not mean pretending the concern was solved. It means avoiding an argument
after the relevant answer has been given. Acknowledge, answer, return to the decision.

### Indifference, sheep factor and impulse

Indifference lowers pressure because the salesperson does not appear to need the result.
Sheep factor raises social proof by showing that other people have acted. Impulse shortens the
distance between interest and action.

These tools are powerful together. "Many local families are already helping, it starts in six
weeks, and you can contribute for as long as you can" communicates social proof, a time point
and a low-commitment path.

It can also move someone faster than they can properly evaluate the request.

The clean boundary is this: resolve uncertainty, do not manufacture confusion. Ask for a
decision, but do not treat a decision as defective because it is no. The customer should know
the amount, timing, duration, cancellation route and destination of the money before agreeing.

### A practical objection test

Before rehashing, ask yourself:

1. Is this a missing fact I can answer?
2. Is this a practical obstacle that can be changed?
3. Is this a trust issue that needs verification rather than persuasion?
4. Has the person already given a clear refusal?

The first three may justify another sentence. The fourth ends the sale.

## 6. How to learn faster than the office can teach you

The notebook calls the mentor a cheat code.

The student's responsibilities were to ask questions, take notes, practise, give full effort,
stay positive inwardly and outwardly, and maintain a strong work ethic. The mentor had to set
goals, live as an example, hold people accountable, ask questions, build networks and observe
without bias.

That last responsibility matters. A mentor who needs the trainee's numbers may not be an
unbiased observer. Good coaching separates the person's development from the coach's short
term gain.

### Become an information seeker

Phil Reid's office visit produced one of the clearest development instructions in the notes:
move from information receiver to information seeker.

Do not wait for the morning meeting to contain exactly what you need. Find somebody with more
sales, stronger sign-ups or a better team. Travel to other offices. Watch how stronger people
open, pause and close. Ask what they learnt late that they wish they had known early.

One note says, "Not what I say, but who I say it to." The same question asked of a random
person and of someone who repeatedly produces the target result does not have equal value.
Listen widely, but weigh evidence.

The formal development environments were the office, coaches meeting, prime time and field.
The extra opportunities were one-to-ones, conference calls, office visits, breakfast meetings,
social events and industry events. The October plan added road trips every six weeks and a
team activity every two months. The point was not only to work the field but to reaffirm and
extend knowledge.

### The skill gap

The skill gap is the distance between your current behaviour and the behaviour required for
the result. Getting good at sales takes as long as it takes to learn and apply the missing
skills.

This sounds obvious, but it is better than vague motivation. If the gap is pace, record the
pitch and time it. If the gap is weak closing, count how many full presentations end without a
direct ask. If the gap is product knowledge, list the questions you could not answer. If the
gap is resilience, measure whether effort falls after rejection.

"Be personally aggressive towards getting better" makes sense here. Attack the defined gap,
not yourself.

### Questions for stronger performers

My notes kept a question list for high rollers:

- Describe a challenge and how you overcame it.
- What did you learn over time that you did not know you would need?
- How do you motivate yourself?
- Which sales techniques produce your best results?
- Give an example of a team member who developed successfully.
- Which skills are essential?
- How do you turn objections into sales without losing quality?

The answers should become experiments, not scripture. Copy the observable behaviour, test it
on your site and keep the result.

## 7. Training people and building a culture

My main October goal was written bluntly: build a team of five tough guys.

The notebook had already moved beyond individual selling. Systems had to be teachable and
copyable. Train people correctly from the beginning so they have a good reference to imitate.
Spend time with the team. Phil had anxiety, the note says, but the team motivated him.

The proposed new-starter plan expanded by stage. In the first week: two days of in-house
training, a breakfast meeting, a social night and a book. By the second week: the same base,
plus a conference call and social activity. By the fourth week: office visits and road trips
as well.

There is sense in this. A new person needs instruction, observation, practice and belonging.
The field is emotionally punishing when every rejection feels private. A team gives the day a
shared rhythm.

### Impacts

Morning talks, called impacts, followed three questions:

1. What are we doing?
2. Why are we doing it?
3. How are we doing it?

That structure forces a motivational talk to contain an action. It can be used to set a site
target, teach one part of the pitch or review yesterday's numbers.

Fun challenges were another tool. "Have one hundred people say no to you today" reframes
rejection as evidence of action. It can help a trainee who avoids approaches. It should not be
used to excuse poor qualification or harassment.

### Culture can support or capture

My notes say to build a culture of hard work, confidence and positivity because trained people
will carry it on. A positive atmosphere improves morale. People are less likely to quit and
more likely to work long hours.

That final sentence changes the meaning.

Culture can help people do difficult work. It can also make leaving feel like betrayal. Social
nights, road trips, breakfast meetings and mentor relationships join work, friendship and
identity. The office no longer has to issue a direct command if the worker has learnt to police
themselves.

The interview tactics in my 4 November note show the system at its worst. Throw the candidate
on the back foot so they feel inferior. Ask for a paper CV and write a number other than one on
it, creating the false impression that several people have already been interviewed. Make the
candidate sell themselves.

That is not a clever recruitment method. It is staged scarcity and deception. A company that
has to manufacture status in the first meeting is already teaching the candidate the wrong
lesson about sales.

A mentor should build judgement, not dependence. A culture should survive questions. A system
worth copying should not need lies.

## 8. Goals, value and progression

Goals in the notebook begin with three questions: who sets them, who writes them down and who
reviews them?

If the office sets the goal, the worker repeats it and nobody reviews the result honestly, it
is not a goal. It is a slogan.

The weekly structure used four categories:

1. Sales goal.
2. Development goal.
3. Personal goal.
4. Career goal.

The sales goal measures output. The development goal names a skill. The personal goal stops
work from consuming the whole planning system. The career goal connects the week to a longer
direction.

### The eight daily steps

The recovered notebook gives the authoritative list:

1. Positive attitude.
2. Time management.
3. Be prepared.
4. Give 100 percent effort.
5. Know the site.
6. Take action.
7. Have a target.
8. Take control.

These are not the five stages of a pitch. They are conditions for running the day. Later,
ChatGPT tried to reconstruct the list and invented different steps. That is why the notebook
matters.

### GRASP

GRASP was another development shorthand:

- Get with people. Network with those who can teach or open an opportunity.
- Responsibility. Take accountability and fix problems.
- Attitude. Protect the state brought into the field.
- Systems. Make the work repeatable.
- Profitability. Produce a result that can support the activity.

The order is revealing. Profitability comes after people, responsibility, attitude and
systems. The office sold development first because a new seller may not yet have profitable
evidence.

### The business advancement story

My notes from Nicci Flynn's business advancement meeting begin with "there is a method in the
madness". The meeting asked why someone would avoid starting a business: risk, starting
capital, responsibility and the need to research the field. It then asked why someone would
do it anyway: money, lifestyle and sometimes the urge to prove other people wrong.

The office answer was that your reason for success had to be bigger than any bad day. That is
useful when it keeps a temporary setback in proportion. It becomes circular when every doubt
is labelled a bad day and the opportunity itself is never allowed to be wrong. A serious
business goal includes the right to reject a bad business.

### Value and results

"Your value is your result" appears in the early notes. So does the instruction to separate
value from money because money follows results, and results follow skill, knowledge, habits
and work ethic.

The narrow version is correct. Professional claims need evidence. A person who says they are
a strong seller should be able to show quality and quantity over enough attempts.

The broad version is destructive. A bad field day does not reduce a person's human value. A
new seller is not worthless because the system has not yet trained them. A team member should
not have to earn basic respect by producing sign-ups.

Use results to judge a method and a professional claim. Do not use them as a complete theory
of a person.

## 9. Two pitches from the field

The October pitches show the notebook becoming speech.

They are presented as historical case studies. Every statistic, funding claim and programme
detail must be checked against dated charity sources before publication or reuse.

### GOSH Children's Cancer Centre

The GOSH pitch opened with speed: "Hi, just before you dash. Great Ormond Street Children's
Hospital. Have you heard of us?"

If the answer was yes, the reply was: "Brilliant. I'll keep this thirty seconds."

The structure then moved through childhood cancer, the proposed centre and the funding ask.
The strongest section made the building visible. Research, treatment and recovery would sit
under one roof. There would be a school, accommodation for parents and a rooftop garden and
playground. Children could go outside, play in the sun and breathe fresh air. The pitch called
that a return of normality.

The close reduced a very large project to a small household decision: a couple of pounds a
week, beginning in about six weeks, for as long as the donor wished. It also said the campaign
was restricted so the donation would go towards the centre.

Mechanically, the pitch used:

- recognition at the opening;
- a time promise;
- a severe problem;
- an agreement question;
- a physical solution;
- family detail;
- progress already made;
- a small recurring amount;
- a direct close.

The pitch is long for the promised thirty seconds. That mismatch matters. A time promise is
part of trust. Either shorten the body or stop promising a duration the script cannot meet.

### Acorns Children's Hospice

The Acorns notes moved from a recognised national hospital to "your local children's
hospice". Locality was the central reason to care.

The draft said demand for end-of-life care had risen, thousands of children might still need
help and the loss of a government grant had created a funding gap. It added what hospice care
looked like: families could come to one of three hospices, children could stay overnight and
eat there, and there were visits to a farm. The service was not reduced to the moment of
death. It covered good times and bad.

The close asked for a few pounds a week, compared the amount with a Daysaver and stressed that
the support remained in the community. It began in six weeks and lasted for however long the
person could contribute.

The local frame is effective because it answers a question donors often have: where does my
money go? The overnight stay and farm are effective because they replace a general word such
as "care" with something a family actually experiences.

The risk lies in escalation. "We have never had to turn a child away, but may have to for the
first time" is emotionally severe. It must be demonstrably true, current and properly
qualified. The fact that a line closes sales does not prove it belongs in a pitch.

### What these pitches taught me

A field pitch needs structure, but structure is not the same as a script recited at somebody.
The customer should be able to interrupt. The salesperson should be able to answer. A good
story makes the proposition clear. A bad story makes the listener responsible for relieving
the seller's emotion.

The goal is not to remove feeling. A children's hospice is emotional. The goal is to make
feeling answer to fact.

## Conclusion

I left direct sales with more than a set of closes.

I learnt that confidence can be procedural. Prepare, approach, ask, listen, close and review.
You do not have to wait until you feel fearless. I learnt that rejection becomes less personal
when it is placed inside a large enough sample. I learnt to search for stronger people rather
than waiting to be taught. I learnt that a spreadsheet can correct a story you have started
telling yourself about a good or bad day.

I also learnt to watch the person selling me the opportunity.

Be careful when the company speaks more clearly about promotion, recruitment and ownership
than it does about the customer. Ask where revenue originates. Ask whether progression
depends on selling a product to genuine outside customers or mainly on bringing in more
sellers. Ask about entry fees, required purchases, recurring charges, refunds, cancellations
and the outcome of an ordinary recruit rather than the person on stage.

Commission-only work is not automatically a pyramid or an MLM. Team structures are not proof
either. The warning signs accumulate:

- recruitment is the main route to meaningful income;
- the product is vague, overpriced or secondary to the opportunity;
- people must pay, subscribe or buy inventory to remain eligible;
- earnings stories are vivid but typical numbers are hard to obtain;
- questions about costs are answered with questions about belief;
- friends and family are treated as an initial market to be harvested;
- leaving means losing both income and the social group;
- leaders use lifestyle displays as evidence while avoiding verifiable accounts.

If you cannot explain who the external customer is and why they buy without the recruitment
opportunity attached, stop.

My first conclusion called the book a memorial. I still like the word. A memorial keeps
something because its period has ended. It does not require pretending that every part of the
period was good.

The useful inheritance is practical: work hard, remain a student, track the numbers, control
your delivery, seek better information and ask for the decision. The rest has to earn its
place.
